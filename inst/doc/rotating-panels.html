Rotating panels: net change, chaining and gross flows

A continuous household survey with a rotating design measures the same units more than once. That is what makes the change between two periods far more precise than the levels themselves, because the shared sample cancels part of the sampling error, and it is also what makes the change harder to estimate. The two samples are not independent, so the variance of the change is not the sum of the two variances:

\[V\!\left(\hat\theta_t - \hat\theta_{t-1}\right) = V\!\left(\hat\theta_t\right) + V\!\left(\hat\theta_{t-1}\right) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(\hat\theta_t, \hat\theta_{t-1}\right)\]

and that covariance term is the whole problem. It is not a design constant you can look up: it has to be produced by the way the replicates are drawn.

This vignette covers the three things a rotating panel makes possible, in the order an office needs them:

  1. the net change between two periods, with its honest variance;
  2. chaining period after period the way production actually runs, one month at a time, with only a small object traveling between runs;
  3. the gross flows, i.e. who moved between states, which need a longitudinal weight.

The package’s panel layer follows the methodology of Statistics Canada’s Labour Force Survey (cat. 71-526-X, sec. 7.2.2) for the coordinated replication, and ECLAC’s household-survey manual (chapters XVI and XVII) for the longitudinal weight and the flows.

The structure comes first

Before any weighting, panel_design() reads the unit by wave crossing and describes what is actually there. It computes nothing about weights.

Two things to read in that output.

The overlap matrix is observed, from the data. The profile implied by the pattern is theoretical, derived from the rotation calendar. The declared pattern is therefore verification, not configuration: when the observed overlap falls below what the design implies, the linkage key is suspect, and PN-01 says so. Ordinary attrition pulls the observed overlap down a little; a broken key pulls it down a lot.

The pattern is parsed into the whole profile, not just the adjacent lag, because the informative lag is not always lag 1:

prof <- function(p) round(weightflow:::.wf_pattern_overlap(p)$profile[1:5], 3)
rbind(`6        (Canada LFS, ECH Uruguay)` = prof("6"),
      `4(0)1    (ECLAC ch. XVI example)`   = prof("4(0)1"),
      `2-(2)-2  (Chile ENE, Italy)`        = prof("2-(2)-2"),
      `4-8-4    (US CPS)`                  = prof("4-8-4"),
      `1(2)5    (PNAD Continua)`           = prof("1(2)5"))
#>                                        1     2    3     4     5
#> 6        (Canada LFS, ECH Uruguay) 0.833 0.667 0.50 0.333 0.167
#> 4(0)1    (ECLAC ch. XVI example)   0.750 0.500 0.25 0.000 0.000
#> 2-(2)-2  (Chile ENE, Italy)        0.500 0.000 0.25 0.500 0.250
#> 4-8-4    (US CPS)                  0.750 0.500 0.25 0.000 0.000
#> 1(2)5    (PNAD Continua)           0.000 0.000 0.80 0.000 0.000

Three readings that the full profile makes visible and a single number would hide. Under 2-(2)-2 the lag 4 overlap equals the lag 1 overlap, so a year on year change needs as much coordination as a quarter on quarter one. Under 1(2)5 the adjacent overlap is zero and the informative lag is 3, so a chain built around “the previous quarter” is exactly backwards for the PNAD. And the cycle is not the number of groups in sample: 2-(2)-2 has four groups in the field at any time but a cycle six periods long.

The first two rows are the ones to read against each other:

Both notations are accepted and describe the same calendar: "2-(2)-2" and "2(2)2" are the same design, as are the EU-LFS block notation and ECLAC’s n(m)k.

The declared scheme is checked against the data in both directions. PN-01 fires when the observed overlap falls below what the scheme implies, which points at a linkage key that is not stable between waves. PN-07 fires when two periods the scheme declares disjoint turn out to share sample. Attrition can only remove overlap, never add it, so a surplus means the declared scheme and the data disagree.

Net change between two periods

The coordinated bootstrap resamples PSUs, and a PSU present in both waves is resampled the same way in both. That is what lets the overlap show up as covariance.

w1 <- subset(panel_ine, wave == 1 & disposition == "R")
w2 <- subset(panel_ine, wave == 2 & disposition == "R")
rec <- function(d) weighting_spec(d, base_weights = pw) |>
  step_nonresponse(respondent = disposition == "R", by = "sex")

wb <- wave_bootstrap(list(T1 = rec(w1), T2 = rec(w2)), replicates = 60,
                     strata = "stratum", psu = "psu", seed = 1, progress = FALSE)
change_mean(wb, "unemployed")
#> <weightflow net change>
#>   T1 -> T2
#>   change     : -0.0149512   SE 0.0106493
#>   95% CI    : [-0.0358235, 0.00592097]
#>   V1 0.0001094 | V2 0.0001119 | Cov 5.395e-05 | rho 0.488   (levels)
#>   V = 0.0001134  vs  V1+V2 = 0.0002213  (deff_change 0.512: overlap saved 49%)

rho is the correlation the overlap induces, and deff_change is

\[\mathrm{deff}_\Delta = \frac{V\!\left(\hat\theta_t - \hat\theta_{t-1}\right)}{V\!\left(\hat\theta_t\right) + V\!\left(\hat\theta_{t-1}\right)}\]

the ratio between the variance reported here and what an office would report if it treated the two periods as independent samples. A deff_change near 0.5 means the overlap has paid for half the variance of the change. Ignoring it does not give a conservative answer, it gives a wrong one, in either direction depending on the sign of the covariance.

Chaining: how production actually runs

wave_bootstrap() wants every wave at once. An office does not have them: it publishes month t weeks after month t-1, and the twelfth month of the year cannot wait for the first eleven to be reprocessed. wave_step() runs one period at a time, and hands the next period a small object, the carry, that is all it needs.

Here is a three period chain, run the way production runs it: each period separately, writing its carry to disk, with nothing else surviving between runs.

The estimands are declared, not programmed

What to estimate, over which domains and on which subuniverse is declared with the same DSL as the rest of the package, and the plan is handed to wave_step() whole:

dk   <- subset(panel_ine, wave == 1 & disposition == "R")
plan <- rec(dk) |>
  step_domain(sex) |>                                   # domains of study
  step_estimate(mean(unemployed), label = "unemp")      # the estimand

plan
#> <weightflow estimation pipeline>
#>   source     : coordinated bootstrap (0 waves)
#>   domains    : sex
#>   estimands  : 1
#>     - unemp  (over = level)
#>   -> collect_estimates() to evaluate

step_filter() would add a subuniverse (working age population, the employed, households with a female head). It masks rows rather than deleting them, so the coordinated replicate structure stays intact.

This matters here more than it does in a single period. The carry stores the R replicate values of every estimand under its label, and the next period matches them by name. An estimand added halfway through the chain does not exist in the earlier carry, so there are no replicate pairs to estimate a covariance from. wave_step() says so rather than reporting a change as if the two periods were independent:

d1 <- subset(panel_ine, wave == 1 & disposition == "R")
d2 <- subset(panel_ine, wave == 2 & disposition == "R")
one <- function(d) rec(d) |> step_estimate(mean(unemployed), label = "unemp")
two <- function(d) one(d) |> step_estimate(mean(age), label = "mean_age")   # declared late

s1 <- wave_step(rec(d1), estimands = one(d1), replicates = 20, strata = "stratum",
                psu = "psu", period = "t1", seed = 1, progress = FALSE)
s2 <- try(wave_step(rec(d2), previous = wave_carry(s1), estimands = two(d2),
                    replicates = 20, strata = "stratum", psu = "psu",
                    period = "t2", seed = 2, progress = FALSE), silent = TRUE)
#> Warning: carry 't1' has no replicate values for 1 estimand(s) (mean_age), so no
#> change is reported for them. Declare the same `estimands`/`by` in every period
#> of the chain, or re-run that period.

The chain

store <- file.path(tempdir(), "carry"); dir.create(store, showWarnings = FALSE)
month <- c("2026-01", "2026-02", "2026-03")

for (k in 1:3) {
  dk   <- subset(panel_ine, wave == k & disposition == "R")
  plan <- rec(dk) |> step_domain(sex) |> step_estimate(mean(unemployed), label = "unemp")
  prev <- lapply(rev(list.files(store, full.names = TRUE)), readRDS)   # what is on disk
  out  <- wave_step(rec(dk), previous = if (length(prev)) prev else NULL,
                    estimands = plan, replicates = 60, strata = "stratum", psu = "psu",
                    period = month[k], seed = k, progress = FALSE)
  saveRDS(wave_carry(out), file.path(store, paste0(month[k], ".rds")))
}

list.files(store)
#> [1] "2026-01.rds" "2026-02.rds" "2026-03.rds"

Three properties worth stating plainly.

The cross-sectional weights are untouched. Coordination adds the change and the carry; it never moves the point estimate the office publishes.

all.equal(out$weights, prep(rec(subset(panel_ine, wave == 3 & disposition == "R")))$final_weight)
#> [1] TRUE

previous is a list, not a file. Which earlier periods share sample with this one is decided by the rotation calendar, as the figure above shows. Each PSU inherits its multiplicity from the most recent carry that contains it, so gaps and returning cohorts resolve themselves.

The coordination is exact when the stratum keeps its size. Coordination transfers the PSU multiplicities, and when n_h is unchanged the transfer is a permutation, case (ii) of the LFS methodology, so no replicate needs adjusting:

out$strata
#>   stratum n_psu inherited fresh case coordinated
#> 1       1    10        10     0    i           1
#> 2       2    10        10     0    i           1
#> 3       3    10        10     0    i           1
#> 4       4    10        10     0    i           1
#> 5       5    10        10     0    i           1
#> 6       6    10        10     0    i           1

case is the coordination case per stratum and coordinated is the share of replicates that closed without adjustment. That column is the quality indicator that decides whether a figure is publishable, and the four cases are set out in vignette("coordinated-replication").

What has to be saved

This is the question an office asks first, because it decides the production architecture. The answer is: one object per period, and it is small.

cy <- readRDS(file.path(store, "2026-03.rds"))
names(cy)[!vapply(cy, is.null, logical(1))]
#> [1] "period"      "seq"         "mult"        "nh"          "psu_stratum"
#> [6] "theta"       "point"       "data_fp"     "meta"

c(carry_KB  = round(file.size(file.path(store, "2026-03.rds")) / 1024, 1),
  period_rows = nrow(subset(panel_ine, wave == 3 & disposition == "R")))
#>    carry_KB period_rows 
#>         5.1      1718.0

Four kilobytes against a period file of some seventeen hundred rows, and no person level data in it at all: $data, $reps and $weights are NULL in a thin carry. It is a sufficient statistic for the coordination, not a copy of the survey. That matters for more than disk: a carry can be moved between systems and kept for years without carrying a disclosure problem with it.

A recipe ending in step_cre() is the exception. Its control totals are estimated with the previous wave, so replicate b of period t has to rebuild them from replicate b of period t-1, which means the previous period’s replicate weights have to travel too. carry = "auto" detects it, and the object grows accordingly:

thin <- wave_step(rec(w1), estimands = one(w1), replicates = 60, strata = "stratum",
                  psu = "psu", period = "t1", seed = 1, progress = FALSE)
fat  <- wave_step(rec(w1), estimands = one(w1), replicates = 60, strata = "stratum",
                  psu = "psu", period = "t1", seed = 1, carry = "fat", progress = FALSE)

f <- function(x) { p <- tempfile(); saveRDS(wave_carry(x), p); round(file.size(p) / 1024, 1) }
c(thin_KB = f(thin), fat_KB = f(fat))
#> thin_KB  fat_KB 
#>     4.0   160.7

Forty times larger, and it holds the microdata. Keep it under the same rules as the microdata itself.

Combinations over the chain

wave_step() reports pairwise changes. A rolling quarter is not pairwise, and wave_contrast() estimates any linear combination straight from the saved carries, without the waves being in memory:

tr <- lapply(file.path(store, paste0(month, ".rds")), readRDS)

wave_contrast(tr, "unemp")[, c("estimand", "estimate", "se", "lo", "hi")]
#>   estimand estimate         se       lo       hi
#> 1    unemp 0.275714 0.01057827 0.254981 0.296447
wave_contrast(tr, "unemp", contrast = c(-1, 0, 1))[, c("estimand", "estimate", "se")]
#>   estimand    estimate         se
#> 1    unemp 0.001005467 0.01294508

That second line is the change from January to March, computed from three files on disk, and it is the same number the chain already reported in $change. It works because each carry stores the R replicate values of every declared estimand, and those replicates are paired across periods: the coordination transferred the multiplicities. Stacking them into an R by W matrix recovers the whole covariance matrix, and any contrast follows from it as V(a'theta) = a' Sigma a:

S <- vapply(tr, function(p) p$theta[["unemp"]], numeric(60))
colnames(S) <- month
signif(stats::cov(S), 3)
#>          2026-01  2026-02  2026-03
#> 2026-01 1.55e-04 1.09e-04 7.81e-05
#> 2026-02 1.09e-04 1.49e-04 8.43e-05
#> 2026-03 7.81e-05 8.43e-05 1.72e-04

The covariances fall off with the lag, as the overlap profile says they should.

The composite estimator

When the recipe ends in step_cre(), the calibration of period \(t\) targets two blocks at once: the known demographic totals \(\mathbf{X}\), and composite totals \(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\) estimated with the previous wave. Treating the second block as if it were known makes the variance anticonservative, which is why the fat carry exists. $n_cre_injected and $n_cre_skipped audit that the injection happened, and a recipe that points at a period missing from the chain is refused rather than quietly given the wrong one.

The estimator, its two imputations for the birth rotation group and the tuning constant \(\alpha\) are in vignette("composite-estimation").

Gross flows: who moved

A net change is a difference of aggregates. It cannot distinguish an immobile population from one where equal numbers enter and leave employment. For that you need the longitudinal file and a longitudinal weight. It is a second branch off the same microdata and the same cross-sectional recipe, and it does not take part in the chain: it produces no carry and consumes none.

wide <- panel_merge(list(T1 = subset(panel_ine, wave == 1),
                         T2 = subset(panel_ine, wave == 2)),
                    by = c("household_id", "person_no"), require = "all")

lw <- weighting_spec(wide, base_weights = pw_T1) |>
  step_drop_ineligible(disposition_T2 == "OS", reason = "left the target population") |>
  step_attrition(respondent = disposition_T2 == "R", method = "propensity",
                 formula = ~ age_T1 + sex_T1) |>
  prep()

transition_matrix(lw, from = "lf_status_T1", to = "lf_status_T2", format = "row")
#> Warning: The flow table covers 96.2% of the weight: 3.8% with a missing state.
#> Those units are not in any cell, so the conditional and joint formats
#> renormalize over the rest. In a panel that share is usually the attrition --
#> decide it explicitly (an 'out of scope' state, or step_drop_ineligible()).
#> <weightflow transition: lf_status_T1 -> lf_status_T2  [row]>
#>        to
#> from       emp inact  unemp
#>   emp   0.9547     0 0.0453
#>   inact 0.0000     1 0.0000
#>   unemp 0.1120     0 0.8880

boot_transition() adds a standard error per cell, and boot_flows() gives the same information as population totals plus the net flows i->j minus j->i and the margins, i.e. how many started in each state, ended in each, stayed, and moved.

A caveat about the illustration, not the method: the four shipped panel datasets are synthetic, and in all of them inactivity is an absorbing state, so nobody enters or leaves it. The matrix above therefore shows movement only between employment and unemployment, and the inact row and column are degenerate. The mechanics are the point here; on real microdata the transitions in and out of inactivity are usually the interesting ones.

Note the order of the two steps, which is not cosmetic. Leaving the target population is not nonresponse. Someone who died or emigrated is removed from the universe with step_drop_ineligible(), and their weight is not redistributed to anyone. Someone who is still in the universe but did not answer is attrition, and their weight is redistributed, among the units that remain. Collapsing the two inflates the population.

Three further conventions govern a longitudinal weight: who belongs to the longitudinal population, which period’s totals to calibrate to, and why cross-sectional estimates from a longitudinal file are reference only. They are decisions the analyst makes and the package cannot check, and they are set out in vignette("panel-longitudinal") and in ?step_cross_sectional.
